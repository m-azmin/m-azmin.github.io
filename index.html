Tracking hunting dogs where there is no network

A hunter following several dogs through dense forest needs to see where each one is, make a collar beep to find it, and keep receiving positions when a ridge cuts the radio link. Commercial products either rely on a cellular subscription or stop at two dogs with no relay.

During my R&D internship at Num'axes I designed, built and validated a prototype that does all of this over LoRa, peer to peer: no gateway, no smartphone, no network operator. I wrote the radio protocol and the firmware for both the collars and the handheld remotes.

Prototype characteristics
RadioLoRa peer to peer, 869.4 MHz (EU868), SF7, 125 kHz, CR 4/5, 14 dBm
Group sizeUp to 9 collars and 3 remotes, no infrastructure
Position frame8 bytes, 41.2 ms on air (was 11 bytes, 51.5 ms: −19.9%)
Command frame4 bytes, 36.1 ms on air (was 7 bytes, 41.2 ms: −12.4%)
Channel loadG ≈ 0.068 with a full group, about 87% first-try delivery (pure ALOHA model)
HardwareMicrochip WLR089U0, u-blox MAX-M10S, LIS2DW12
FirmwareC, bare-metal, event-driven; one codebase built for both roles
Estimated autonomyCollar ≈ 117 h, remote ≈ 27 h on 2000 mAh (prototype, sleep modes not yet enabled)

A protocol sized for airtime

I started with verbose frames for debugging, then designed compact v2 frames: positions packed as signed 24-bit coordinates (10−4 degree) with a fix flag, commands tagged with a sequence number. Every command (beep, vibration, mute, volume) is acknowledged end to end; duplicates are recognized and re-acknowledged without being executed twice. Pairing only accepts requests inside a time window.

Relaying without flooding the channel

The owner's remote picks a helper from how recently it heard the dog and how close it is by GNSS. Commands go through a request, offer, grant and forward handshake, with the acknowledgement routed back to the owner. Positions are forwarded under a time-limited lease. If the best helper times out, the next candidate is tried.

Reading a competitor's radio from its SPI bus

To ground our design choices, I measured a competing tracker's channels on a spectrum analyzer, then tapped the SPI bus between its microcontroller and LoRa transceiver with a logic analyzer. Decoding the register writes against the transceiver datasheet revealed its real settings (SF8, 62.5 kHz). That analysis is why we chose SF7 at 125 kHz: shorter airtime, better suited to many nodes.

An immobility alarm that ignores breathing

GNSS drifts too much under tree cover to tell whether a pointing dog has stopped, so the collar uses an accelerometer instead. The algorithm tracks changes between samples to cancel gravity, smooths them with an IIR filter and applies hysteresis: 5 s of stillness raises the alarm, 400 ms of clear movement clears it.

Validated from the logs up

Every event is logged over UART with its radio metrics, and each scenario (pairing, commands, GNSS, relay, alarm) has a reproducible test with pass criteria. A few lines from the test campaign:

Radio configured for P2P 869.4 MHz SF7/BW125/CR4_5
RX len=8 RSSI=-104 SNR=7 | GPSv2 g=3 rid=2 dog=1 fix=1
PAIR IGNORED: window closed
DUP CMD ignored (seq=12)
RELAY: REQ → OFFER → GRANT → FWD → CMD_ACK
GPS_RELAY: REQ → GPS_RELAY: ACK OK → RELAY: GPS_FWD

What I would do next

The remote is the autonomy bottleneck at about 27 h, so the largest gain will come from duty-cycled reception rather than fewer transmissions. After that: a dedicated PCB and enclosure, adaptive GNSS reporting when the dog is still, collision backoff, and a wider field campaign in dense, hilly terrain.
